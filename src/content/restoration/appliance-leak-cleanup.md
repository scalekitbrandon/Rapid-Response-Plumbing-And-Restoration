Appliance leaks are tricky because they rarely look as bad as they are. A washing machine that sprayed for ten minutes or a dishwasher that dripped through a whole cycle can leave a floor that looks almost dry by morning. Meanwhile the water has gone under the cabinets and the flooring. ### Why appliance leaks spread so far

Most appliances sit in tight spaces, pushed against walls and tucked under counters. When one leaks, the water has nowhere to go but down and sideways:

- **Under the cabinets.** The toe kick hides a hollow space that fills with water and stays wet with no airflow.
- **Under vinyl plank and laminate.** Floating floors let water run underneath, where it sits on the slab or soaks the subfloor.
- **Into the wall.** Drywall behind a washer or fridge wicks water upward from the bottom edge.
- **Through the ceiling.** Second-floor laundry rooms, attic air handlers and roof-mounted swamp coolers leak into the rooms below.

### Swamp coolers and AC condensate in the High Desert

Desert homes put a lot of water on the roof and in the attic. Evaporative coolers run a constant water supply through a float valve, and when that valve sticks, the cooler overflows onto the roof and into the ducts and ceiling. Central AC systems produce condensate all summer, and a clogged drain line can overflow the pan and soak the drywall below. Brown rings or sagging spots on a ceiling near a vent are worth a call before the drywall gives way.

### Grey water needs more than drying

Supply-line water is clean. Drain water from a washer or dishwasher is grey water, with soap and food waste in it. That means cleaning and disinfecting the affected area, and in some cases removing carpet pad or insulation that can't be cleaned properly.
